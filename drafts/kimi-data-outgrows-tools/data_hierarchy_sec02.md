## 2. The Geometry of Decisions

Maria opens her laptop at 9:47 a.m. and loads fifty thousand transactions from the previous week — each row a purchase, a swipe, a transfer. Within the hour she has distilled them into twelve numbers per transaction: amount, time since last purchase, merchant category frequency, distance from home, velocity of spending across six hours, and seven more she chose after three years of watching fraud patterns come and go. She types seven lines of Python. The model trains in under a minute. When she validates it against held-out fraud cases, the AUC reads 0.9350[^15^]. She has not written a single rule.

This is the shift that rewired the field. Where the previous era demanded that humans enumerate every condition — if amount > X and time < Y — the machine learning era asks a different question entirely. What geometric shape, drawn through a twelve-dimensional space, best separates the fraudulent points from the legitimate ones? Each transaction becomes a coordinate. Each feature is an axis. The model's job is not to remember rules but to discover a boundary — a surface where, on one side, the probability of fraud exceeds the probability of legitimacy[^4^]. The boundary is not a line or a plane; it is a piecewise, irregular surface sculpted by the data itself, folding through dimensions no human intuition can hold[^5^].

But the boundary only works if the space is well-chosen. Those twelve features are the critical human contribution: the art of transforming raw records into a geometric representation where patterns become separable[^6^]. A raw column labeled "timestamp" carries no meaning until it becomes "hours since last transaction" or "deviation from weekly average." Feature engineering is the act of building a coordinate system in which the machine's search can succeed[^31^]. Without it, the most powerful algorithm wanders a featureless plain.

Why tree models came to dominate this geometry is not a matter of opinion. In peer-reviewed head-to-heads and year after year of Kaggle competitions, tree-based models win so consistently that researchers have stopped asking which algorithm is best and started asking why trees match tabular reality so precisely[^1^][^2^]. In one large-scale study, Random Forest achieved approximately 99.84% accuracy against support vector machines' 92.89% — a gap significant at p < 0.001[^1^]. Gradient-boosted trees powered 37 Kaggle winning solutions in 2024-2025 alone[^2^], and a comprehensive 2025 study across 68 diverse datasets found CatBoost and XGBoost holding median ranks of 2 and 2.5, outperforming nearly every neural network approach[^20^].

Three concrete properties explain the staying power. First, compute efficiency: XGBoost processes one hundred thousand samples in approximately 7.5 seconds on standard hardware[^9^]. The model that the fraud team deploys at noon was trained on morning data. Second, tabular dominance: tree models handle the sparse, categorical, interaction-heavy nature of real-world structured data without the overfitting that plagues neural networks on smaller datasets[^3^][^23^]. Third — and increasingly decisive — compliance: the EU AI Act demands that high-risk financial AI systems justify individual decisions and enable human oversight[^17^][^18^]. SHAP provides mathematically grounded feature attributions for every prediction, decomposing a score into the exact contribution of each input variable[^11^][^33^]. A regulator can ask why a transaction was flagged, and the system answers with precision and speed that black-box neural networks struggle to match.

The mechanism behind this is gradient boosting, and it works by a principle that sounds almost biological. Each tree partitions the feature space along axis-aligned splits — amount > 500, time_since_last < 2 hours — carving the space into rectangular regions[^7^]. But a single tree is weak. The algorithm adds trees sequentially, each trained not to predict the outcome directly but to correct the errors of all previous trees combined. It is gradient descent operating in the space of functions, each new tree predicting the residual — the leftover signal, the mistakes the ensemble still makes[^21^]. The boundary grows more precise with every addition. The algorithm learns weights; it does not write rules. The human built the space. The machine found the boundary.

And then — the crack.

Hand that same model a scanned receipt. A JPEG, 224 by 224 pixels, 150,528 numbers in three color channels. The tree stares at a flat vector of pixel intensities and finds nothing to split on. No edges. No texture gradients. No concept of "amount" or "merchant name." Feature engineering fails here because there is no human-readable transformation that collapses a hundred thousand pixel values into separable coordinates[^38^]. The inductive biases that make trees extraordinary on structured data — axis-aligned splits that isolate the few informative features — become meaningless when every pixel carries a fragment of meaning and only their spatial arrangement matters[^10^]. David Wolpert and William Macready proved this formally in 1996: no algorithm is universally superior. An algorithm's power is always tied to the structure of the problem it faces[^10^]. XGBoost dominates tabular data because its assumptions match tabular reality. On raw pixels, those same assumptions are not merely weak — they are deaf.

It was this exact limit that created the conditions for the next transformation. If you cannot engineer a feature from raw pixels, you must build a machine that engineers them for you.
