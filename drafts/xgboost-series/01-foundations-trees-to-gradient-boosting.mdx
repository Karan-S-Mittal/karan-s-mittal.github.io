In modern statistical learning, tree-based ensembles occupy an unusual and resilient position. Despite the dominance of deep neural networks in perceptual domains such as computer vision and natural language processing, gradient boosted decision trees remain the foundational workhorse for structured, tabular, and heterogeneously-typed data.

Yet the theoretical mechanism powering gradient boosting is often obscured behind software APIs or conflated with heuristic sample reweighting.

To understand why gradient boosting works—and why it achieves such remarkable empirical sample efficiency—we must shift our mathematical vantage point. We must transition from optimizing finite parameter vectors in Euclidean weight space $\mathbb{R}^p$ to performing **steepest gradient descent directly in an infinite-dimensional function space** $\mathcal{H}$.

This essay constructs that paradigm from first principles:
1. **The Recursive Partitioning Primitive:** How decision trees carve continuous feature spaces into orthogonal hyper-rectangles.
2. **The Bias-Variance Fork:** Why Random Forests average independent variance while Boosting sequentially contracts bias.
3. **The Geometric Shift:** How Jerome Friedman reformulated boosting from AdaBoost's sample reweighting into functional gradient descent.
4. **The Derivation of Pseudo-Residuals:** How arbitrary differentiable loss functions project their negative gradients onto constrained base trees.
5. **Mechanics of Regularization:** Why shrinkage (learning rate) and stochastic subsampling govern the geometry of convergence.

## 1. First Principles of Decision Trees: Axis-Aligned Partitioning

Every ensemble algorithm begins with a base hypothesis class. In tree-based modeling, that base learner is the **Classification and Regression Tree (CART)**.

Let our training dataset be $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N$, where each feature vector $\mathbf{x}_i = (x_{i,1}, x_{i,2}, \dots, x_{i,d})^{\mathsf T} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ represents a $d$-dimensional measurement, and $y_i \in \mathbb{R}$ represents the corresponding target label.

### The Formal Tree Representation

Mathematically, a decision tree with $J$ terminal nodes (leaves) partitions the continuous input space $\mathbb{R}^d$ into $J$ disjoint, axis-aligned hyper-rectangular regions $\{R_1, R_2, \dots, R_J\}$ such that:

$$
\bigcup_{j=1}^J R_j = \mathbb{R}^d \quad \text{and} \quad R_j \cap R_k = \emptyset \quad \forall \; j \neq k
$$

The tree assigns a single constant scalar prediction $\gamma_j \in \mathbb{R}$ to every input falling within region $R_j$. The complete mapping $f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ is expressed as a linear combination of indicator functions:

$$
f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{j=1}^J \gamma_j \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{x} \in R_j)
$$

<p class="formula-note"><strong>How to read it.</strong> $\mathbb{I}(\mathbf{x} \in R_j)$ evaluates to $1$ if the input vector $\mathbf{x}$ lands inside hyper-rectangle $R_j$, and $0$ otherwise. Because the regions are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive, exactly one indicator fires for any input, returning that region's constant weight $\gamma_j$.</p>

For a fixed partition $\{R_j\}_{j=1}^J$ under a squared error loss metric $L(y, \hat{y}) = \frac{1}{2}(y - \hat{y})^2$, the optimal leaf response $\gamma_j^*$ is analytically trivial—it is simply the arithmetic mean of all training observations mapped to leaf $R_j$:

$$
\gamma_j^* = \arg\min_{\gamma_j} \sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in R_j} \frac{1}{2}(y_i - \gamma_j)^2 = \bar{y}_j = \frac{1}{N_j} \sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in R_j} y_i
$$

where $N_j = |\{\mathbf{x}_i \in R_j\}|$ denotes the cardinality of training instances falling into leaf $j$.

### Greedy Recursive Binary Splitting (CART)

Finding the globally optimal partition $\{R_j\}_{j=1}^J$ that minimizes the total empirical sum of squared errors across all possible configurations is an **NP-complete combinatorial problem** [1]. 

Practical implementations therefore resort to a **top-down greedy heuristic**:
1. At the current node with training subset $S \subseteq \mathcal{D}$, scan all feature dimensions $j \in \{1, 2, \dots, d\}$.
2. For each feature $j$, scan all candidate split locations $s \in \mathbb{R}$ dividing $S$ into:
   $$
   R_{\text{left}}(j, s) = \{\mathbf{x} \in S \mid x_j \le s\} \quad \text{and} \quad R_{\text{right}}(j, s) = \{\mathbf{x} \in S \mid x_j > s\}
   $$
3. Select the optimal pair $(j^*, s^*)$ that maximizes the impurity reduction:

$$
(j^*, s^*) = \arg\max_{j, s} \left[ \sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in S} (y_i - \bar{y}_S)^2 - \left( \sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in R_{\text{left}}(j,s)} (y_i - \bar{y}_{\text{left}})^2 + \sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in R_{\text{right}}(j,s)} (y_i - \bar{y}_{\text{right}})^2 \right) \right]
$$

<p class="formula-note"><strong>How to read it.</strong> The total variance of the parent node $S$ minus the combined weighted variance of the proposed left and right child subsets represents the variance explained by the split. The greedy search chooses the split that removes the most variance at this single step.</p>

4. Recursively repeat this partitioning on $R_{\text{left}}$ and $R_{\text{right}}$ until a stopping condition (such as maximum depth $D$ or minimum sample count $N_{\min}$) is satisfied.

### The Inherent Flaws of Single Decision Trees

While single decision trees provide non-parametric flexibility, handle heterogeneous features without scaling, and naturally model complex high-order feature interactions, they suffer from severe structural limitations:
- **Axis-Aligned Rigidity:** Because cuts are strictly orthogonal to coordinate axes, approximating a simple diagonal boundary $x_1 + x_2 \ge c$ requires a deep, jagged "staircase" partition containing exponentially many leaves.
- **Extreme High Variance (Instability):** Because CART is hierarchical, a slight perturbation at the root split fundamentally alters all downstream node populations. An error made near the top propagates irrevocably through the entire subtree hierarchy.

## 2. The Bias-Variance Dichotomy: Bagging vs. Boosting

To understand how ensembles tame the instability of decision trees, we examine the canonical **bias-variance decomposition** of the expected generalization error for an estimator $\hat{f}(\mathbf{x})$ under squared loss:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[(y - \hat{f}(\mathbf{x}))^2\right] = \underbrace{\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x})] - f^*(\mathbf{x})\right)^2}_{\operatorname{Bias}^2(\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}))} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\left(\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}) - \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x})]\right)^2\right]}_{\operatorname{Var}(\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}))} + \underbrace{\sigma^2_{\epsilon}}_{\text{Irreducible Noise}}
$$

<p class="formula-note"><strong>How to read it.</strong> Total generalization error decomposes into three additive components: the systematic approximation error (bias), the sensitivity of the learned model to the particular training sample draw (variance), and the irreducible noise of the data-generating process $\sigma_\epsilon^2$.</p>

This decomposition creates a fundamental fork in ensemble design:

| Property | Deep Decision Tree (Depth $\ge 12$) | Shallow Decision Tree / Stump (Depth $1 - 4$) |
|---|---|---|
| **Expressive Capacity** | Very High (overfits noise) | Low (rigid step function) |
| **Bias Profile** | $\operatorname{Bias}^2 \approx 0$ (Low) | $\operatorname{Bias}^2 \gg 0$ (High) |
| **Variance Profile** | $\operatorname{Var} \gg 0$ (High) | $\operatorname{Var} \approx 0$ (Low) |
| **Ensemble Remedy** | **Bagging** (Averaging) | **Boosting** (Iterative Residuals) |

### Why Bagging Cannot Reduce Bias

In **Bootstrap Aggregating (Bagging)** and **Random Forests** [2], we train $B$ independent, deep, unpruned decision trees $\{T_b(\mathbf{x})\}_{b=1}^B$ on bootstrap samples drawn with replacement from $\mathcal{D}$.

The final ensemble prediction is the unweighted average:

$$
F_{\text{bag}}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^B T_b(\mathbf{x})
$$

If each individual tree has variance $\sigma^2$ and any pair of trees shares a positive pairwise correlation $\rho = \operatorname{Corr}(T_j(\mathbf{x}), T_k(\mathbf{x}))$, the variance of the ensemble average evaluates to:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(F_{\text{bag}}(\mathbf{x})) = \rho \sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B} \sigma^2
$$

<p class="formula-note"><strong>How to read it.</strong> As the ensemble size $B \to \infty$, the second term decays to zero, leaving $\rho \sigma^2$. To minimize this residual variance floor, Random Forests introduce random feature subspace sampling ($m = \lfloor\sqrt{d}\rfloor$) at each split to intentionally decorrelate the trees (driving $\rho \to 0$).</p>

However, look at the expected prediction:

$$
\mathbb{E}[F_{\text{bag}}(\mathbf{x})] = \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^B T_b(\mathbf{x})\right] = \mathbb{E}[T_1(\mathbf{x})]
$$

Averaging identical distributions leaves the expectation completely unchanged. **Bagging cannot reduce bias.** If the base learner is underfitted (high bias), the bagged ensemble remains equally underfitted.

### The Boosting Philosophy: Iterative Bias Contraction

Boosting approaches the problem from the opposite direction [3, 4]. Instead of averaging independent deep trees in parallel, boosting constructs an **additive sequence** of shallow, low-variance base learners (often trees of depth $2$ to $6$):

$$
F_M(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{m=0}^M f_m(\mathbf{x})
$$

Each subsequent base model $f_m(\mathbf{x})$ is explicitly chosen to correct the errors committed by the cumulative predecessor ensemble $F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} f_k(\mathbf{x})$. Because each step reduces the remaining approximation error, boosting systematically drives **bias to zero** while maintaining control over variance through constrained tree depth and step-size shrinkage.

## 3. The Geometric Breakthrough: Optimization in Function Space

Early boosting algorithms, most notably **AdaBoost** (Freund & Schapire, 1996), framed boosting as an iterative sample-reweighting procedure [4]. At iteration $m$, misclassified training points received exponential weight increases:

$$
w_i^{(m+1)} = w_i^{(m)} \exp\left(-\alpha_m y_i h_m(\mathbf{x}_i)\right)
$$

While AdaBoost was an undeniable empirical triumph, its mathematical foundations appeared bespoke to binary classification ($y \in \{-1, +1\}$) and exponential loss $L(y, F) = e^{-yF}$. It offered no obvious path toward robust regression, multi-class classification, or custom user-defined loss functions.

In 2001, **Jerome H. Friedman** published a unifying conceptual breakthrough in *The Annals of Statistics*: **Greedy Function Approximation: A Gradient Boosting Machine** [5].

Friedman realized that AdaBoost was not an ad-hoc sample-weighting trick. It was a specific realization of **numerical gradient descent formulated directly in function space**.

### The Mathematical Formulation of Function Space Optimization

Consider the expected risk minimization objective for an arbitrary differentiable loss function $L(y, F(\mathbf{x}))$:

$$
\min_{F} \Phi(F) = \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x}, y)}\left[L(y, F(\mathbf{x}))\right]
$$

In empirical risk minimization over a finite dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N$, we evaluate the function $F$ exclusively at the $N$ observed data points. We can therefore represent the function $F$ as an $N$-dimensional vector in $\mathbb{R}^N$:

$$
\mathbf{f} = \begin{bmatrix} F(\mathbf{x}_1) \\ F(\mathbf{x}_2) \\ \vdots \\ F(\mathbf{x}_N) \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^N
$$

The empirical loss on the training set becomes a function of this vector $\mathbf{f}$:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{f}) = \sum_{i=1}^N L(y_i, F(\mathbf{x}_i)) = \sum_{i=1}^N L(y_i, f_i)
$$

If we apply standard unconstrained gradient descent in $\mathbb{R}^N$, the update at iteration $m$ is:

$$
\mathbf{f}_m = \mathbf{f}_{m-1} - \rho_m \mathbf{g}_m
$$

where $\mathbf{g}_m \in \mathbb{R}^N$ is the gradient of $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{f})$ evaluated at the current state $\mathbf{f}_{m-1}$:

$$
g_{im} = \left[ \frac{\partial L(y_i, F(\mathbf{x}_i))}{\partial F(\mathbf{x}_i)} \right]_{F(\mathbf{x}) = F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x})}
$$

### The Generalization Dilemma and Friedman's Projection

If we simply execute the unconstrained point update $\mathbf{f}_m = \mathbf{f}_{m-1} - \rho_m \mathbf{g}_m$, we minimize loss on the $N$ training observations, but **we learn nothing about how to predict on an unseen point** $\mathbf{x}_{\text{new}} \notin \mathcal{D}$. The function values at points outside the training set remain completely undefined.

> **Friedman's Masterstroke:** To generalize to the entire continuous domain $\mathbb{R}^d$, we must constrain the update vector to lie within a parameterized hypothesis class of base models $\mathcal{F}_{\text{tree}} = \{h(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{a})\}$. We do this by fitting a decision tree $h(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{a}_m)$ to predict the negative gradient values $-g_{im}$ across the training set via least squares:
> 
> $$
> \mathbf{a}_m = \arg\min_{\mathbf{a}, \beta} \sum_{i=1}^N \left( -g_{im} - \beta h(\mathbf{x}_i; \mathbf{a}) \right)^2
> $$

Geometrically, fitting the base decision tree to the negative gradient components is an **orthogonal least-squares projection** of the unconstrained steepest descent vector $-\mathbf{g}_m \in \mathbb{R}^N$ onto the constrained manifold of decision tree functions $\mathcal{F}_{\text{tree}} \subset \mathcal{H}$.

## 4. Formal Derivation: Pseudo-Residuals & Optimal Leaf Weights

We now present the complete, general mathematical derivation of Gradient Boosting for an arbitrary differentiable loss function $L(y, F(\mathbf{x}))$.

### Step 1: Initial Constant Estimation

Initialize the ensemble with a constant function $F_0(\mathbf{x})$ that minimizes the aggregate loss across all training samples:

$$
F_0(\mathbf{x}) = \arg\min_{\gamma} \sum_{i=1}^N L(y_i, \gamma)
$$

### Step 2: The Iterative Boosting Loop

For each boosting iteration $m = 1, 2, \dots, M$:

#### 2.1 Calculate Pseudo-Residuals
Compute the negative gradient of the loss function with respect to the model's current predictions, evaluated at $F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i)$. These values are termed **pseudo-residuals** $r_{im}$:

$$
r_{im} = -\left[ \frac{\partial L(y_i, F(\mathbf{x}_i))}{\partial F(\mathbf{x}_i)} \right]_{F(\mathbf{x}) = F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x})}, \quad \forall \; i \in \{1, 2, \dots, N\}
$$

<p class="formula-note"><strong>How to read it.</strong> $r_{im}$ represents the local directional derivative indicating how increasing the model prediction $F(\mathbf{x}_i)$ changes the loss for sample $i$. The negative sign ensures we move in the direction of steepest loss descent.</p>

#### 2.2 Fit Base Tree to Pseudo-Residuals
Train a CART regression tree using the training set $\{(\mathbf{x}_i, r_{im})\}_{i=1}^N$. This tree partitions the feature space into $J_m$ terminal regions $\{R_{1m}, R_{2m}, \dots, R_{J_m m}\}$.

#### 2.3 Compute Optimal Leaf Weights via Line Search
While the tree structure (the splits) was found by minimizing squared error with respect to the pseudo-residuals $r_{im}$, the predicted value assigned to each leaf must minimize the **original loss function** $L(y, F)$. 

For each terminal leaf $j \in \{1, \dots, J_m\}$, we perform a separate 1D optimization (line search) over the samples landing in region $R_{jm}$:

$$
\gamma_{jm} = \arg\min_{\gamma} \sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in R_{jm}} L\left(y_i, F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i) + \gamma\right)
$$

#### 2.4 Update the Ensemble with Shrinkage
Update the cumulative model by adding the scaled tree predictions, modulated by the learning rate (shrinkage factor) $\eta \in (0, 1]$:

$$
F_m(\mathbf{x}) = F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}) + \eta \sum_{j=1}^{J_m} \gamma_{jm} \mathbb{I}(\mathbf{x} \in R_{jm})
$$

---

### Derivations for Canonical Loss Functions

To see the power of this unified formulation, let us derive the exact pseudo-residuals $r_{im}$ and leaf weights $\gamma_{jm}$ for the three most critical objective functions in machine learning.

#### Case A: Mean Squared Error (L2 Regression)

$$
L(y, F) = \frac{1}{2}(y - F)^2
$$

**1. Pseudo-Residual Derivation:**
$$
r_{im} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial F}\left[\frac{1}{2}(y_i - F)^2\right]_{F=F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i)} = y_i - F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i)
$$

Notice that under L2 loss, the pseudo-residual is **identically equal to the standard algebraic residual** $y_i - \hat{y}_i$.

**2. Leaf Value Optimization:**
$$
\gamma_{jm} = \arg\min_{\gamma} \sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in R_{jm}} \frac{1}{2}\left(y_i - F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i) - \gamma\right)^2 = \frac{1}{|R_{jm}|} \sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in R_{jm}} r_{im}
$$

The optimal leaf update is simply the arithmetic mean of the pseudo-residuals in that leaf.

---

#### Case B: Binary Logistic Loss (Classification with Log-Odds)

For binary classification where $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$, we parameterize the probability via the logistic sigmoid of the real-valued ensemble output $F(\mathbf{x}) = \ln\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)$:

$$
p(\mathbf{x}) = \sigma(F(\mathbf{x})) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-F(\mathbf{x})}}
$$

The negative Bernoulli log-likelihood (cross-entropy) loss is:

$$
L(y, F) = -\left[ y \ln(\sigma(F)) + (1-y) \ln(1 - \sigma(F)) \right] = -y F + \ln(1 + e^F)
$$

**1. Pseudo-Residual Derivation:**
$$
r_{im} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial F}\left[-y_i F + \ln(1 + e^F)\right]_{F=F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i)} = y_i - \frac{e^{F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i)}}{1 + e^{F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i)}} = y_i - p_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i)
$$

The pseudo-residual is the difference between the binary ground truth $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$ and the model's currently predicted probability $p_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i) \in (0, 1)$.

**2. Leaf Value Optimization via Newton-Raphson:**
The exact line search $\arg\min_\gamma \sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in R_{jm}} L(y_i, F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i) + \gamma)$ has no closed-form solution. We approximate it using a single **second-order Taylor expansion (Newton-Raphson step)**:

$$
\gamma_{jm} \approx -\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in R_{jm}} \frac{\partial L}{\partial F}}{\sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in R_{jm}} \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial F^2}} = \frac{\sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in R_{jm}} (y_i - p_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i))}{\sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in R_{jm}} p_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i)(1 - p_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i))}
$$

<p class="formula-note"><strong>How to read it.</strong> The numerator sums the pseudo-residuals (first derivatives), while the denominator sums the variance of the Bernoulli distribution $p(1-p)$ (second derivatives / Hessian). This exact equation forms the foundational leaf weight calculation in XGBoost and LightGBM [6].</p>

---

#### Case C: Mean Absolute Error (L1 Robust Median Regression)

$$
L(y, F) = |y - F|
$$

**1. Pseudo-Residual Derivation:**
$$
r_{im} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial F}|y_i - F|_{F=F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i)} = \operatorname{sign}(y_i - F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i))
$$

The pseudo-residuals depend **only on the sign** of the error ($\pm 1$), completely ignoring error magnitude. This makes L1 gradient boosting exceptionally robust to severe outliers in the target labels.

**2. Leaf Value Optimization:**
$$
\gamma_{jm} = \arg\min_{\gamma} \sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in R_{jm}} |y_i - F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i) - \gamma| = \operatorname{median}_{\mathbf{x}_i \in R_{jm}}\left(y_i - F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i)\right)
$$

The optimal leaf update under L1 loss is the **sample median** of the raw residuals falling into leaf $R_{jm}$.

## 5. Regularization: Shrinkage and Stochastic Subsampling

Because gradient boosting sequentially contracts bias, an unconstrained boosting procedure will eventually overfit the training set, memorizing idiosyncratic noise in the pseudo-residuals. Friedman introduced two crucial regularization mechanisms to control ensemble capacity:

### 1. Shrinkage (The Learning Rate $\eta$)

Instead of adding the full fitted tree to the ensemble, we scale each tree's contribution by a constant factor $\eta \in (0, 1]$:

$$
F_m(\mathbf{x}) = F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}) + \eta f_m(\mathbf{x})
$$

Empirically and theoretically, reducing $\eta$ acts as an $L_2$ regularization penalty on the trajectory length in function space. Setting $\eta < 0.1$ forces the model to take small, conservative steps, allowing subsequent trees to re-estimate gradients from diverse perspectives.

The trade-off is computational: the required number of iterations scales inversely with shrinkage ($M \propto 1/\eta$).

### 2. Stochastic Gradient Boosting (Subsampling)

In 2002, Friedman proposed **Stochastic Gradient Boosting** [7]: at each iteration $m$, draw a random subsample of the training data $\mathcal{S}_m \subset \mathcal{D}$ of fraction $f \in (0, 1)$ without replacement.

Compute pseudo-residuals and fit the base tree $h_m(\mathbf{x})$ using *only* the subsample $\mathcal{S}_m$:
- Injects independent Monte Carlo noise into gradient estimation, preventing individual trees from co-adapting too strongly.
- Reduces per-iteration tree construction time by a factor of $(1 - f)$.
- Provides out-of-bag (OOB) validation estimates at zero marginal cost.

## 7. Comparative Architectural Synthesis

To conclude our first-principles analysis, we compare the mechanical and operational characteristics of the three dominant paradigms in tree-based machine learning:

| Dimension | Single CART Tree | Random Forest (Bagging) | Gradient Boosted Trees |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Base Learner Complexity** | Deep ($D \ge 12$) | Deep, unpruned ($D \ge 15$) | Shallow ($D = 2 \text{ to } 6$) |
| **Ensemble Mechanism** | None (Standalone) | Parallel bootstrap averaging | Sequential functional gradient descent |
| **Primary Error Targeted** | Neither (High variance) | **Variance** ($\rho \sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2$) | **Bias** (Residual gradient steps) |
| **Loss Function Flexibility** | Squared error / Gini / Entropy | Squared error / Gini / Entropy | **Any differentiable loss** $L(y, F)$ |
| **Optimization Space** | Feature splits in $\mathbb{R}^d$ | Feature splits in $\mathbb{R}^d$ | **Function space** $\mathcal{H} = \{F: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}\}$ |
| **Sensitivity to Outliers** | Moderate | Low (Averaged across trees) | High with L2; **Immune with L1/Huber** |
| **Key Hyperparameters** | `max_depth`, `min_samples_split` | `n_estimators`, `max_features` | `learning_rate` ($\eta$), `n_estimators` ($M$), `max_depth` |
