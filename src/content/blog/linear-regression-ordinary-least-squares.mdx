When we train modern deep neural networks with billions of parameters, the fundamental engine driving parameter updates is conceptually identical to what Carl Friedrich Gauss and Adrien-Marie Legendre formulated over two centuries ago: **minimizing the sum of squared discrepancies between observations and model predictions**.

Before dealing with non-convex neural loss surfaces, multi-head attention projections, or deep gradient descent trajectories, we must establish complete mathematical and geometric intuition for the atomic unit of supervised learning: **Simple Linear Regression and the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) closed-form solution**.

---

## 1. Anatomy of a Residual: Why "Least Squares"?

Suppose we observe $n$ data points in two dimensions:

$$\mathcal{D} = \{(x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2), \dots, (x_n, y_n)\}$$

We hypothesize that $y$ has a linear relationship with $x$, parameterized by a slope $m$ and an intercept $b$:

$$\hat{y}_i = m x_i + b$$

For any candidate line, our prediction $\hat{y}_i$ will rarely match the true observed value $y_i$ exactly. The vertical discrepancy is called the **residual** ($e_i$):

$$e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i = y_i - (m x_i + b)$$

```
        y ▲
          │                 (x_i, y_i)  ◄── Actual observation
          │                     ●
          │                     │
          │     Residual e_i    │  ◄── Vertical discrepancy
          │                     │
          │  ───────────────────o────────  Line: ŷ = mx + b
          │                 (x_i, ŷ_i)  ◄── Predicted point
          │
          └────────────────────────────────► x
```

### Why Square the Residuals?

If we simply added the raw residuals together ($\sum e_i$), positive residuals above the line would cancel out negative residuals below the line. A line passing through $(0, 100)$ and $(10, -100)$ could have $\sum e_i = 0$ despite missing every single data point by miles.

To penalize all errors strictly positively, we could consider:
1. **Absolute Deviations ($|e_i|$)**: Leads to Median Regression ($L_1$ loss). However, $|e_i|$ is not differentiable at $e_i = 0$, requiring linear programming rather than clean calculus.
2. **Squared Deviations ($e_i^2$)**: Leads to Mean Squared Error ($L_2$ loss). 
   - **Geometric Meaning**: Each residual forms the side of a literal square box of area $e_i^2$.
   - **Penalization**: Disproportionately penalizes large outliers ($4^2 = 16$, while $1^2 = 1$).
   - **Smooth Convexity**: The sum $\sum e_i^2$ is a smooth, continuously differentiable parabolic bowl with a single, unique global minimum.

The objective function to minimize is the **Sum of Squared Residuals (SSR)**:

$$J(m, b) = \text{SSR} = \sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \Big(y_i - (m x_i + b)\Big)^2$$

## 3. The Mathematical Derivation: Finding the Minimum via Calculus

To find the parameters $(m^*, b^*)$ that minimize $J(m, b)$, we compute the partial derivatives of $J$ with respect to $m$ and $b$, and set them to zero:

$$\frac{\partial J}{\partial m} = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{\partial J}{\partial b} = 0$$

### Step A: Derivative with Respect to Intercept ($b$)

Applying the chain rule:

$$\frac{\partial J}{\partial b} = \sum_{i=1}^n 2\Big(y_i - (mx_i + b)\Big)(-1) = -2 \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - mx_i - b) = 0$$

Dividing by $-2n$:

$$\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n y_i - m\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\right) - b = 0$$

Recognizing the sample means $\bar{y} = \frac{1}{n}\sum y_i$ and $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i$:

$$\bar{y} - m\bar{x} - b = 0 \implies \mathbf{b^* = \bar{y} - m^*\bar{x}}$$

> **Core Geometric Law**: The optimal regression line **must always pass through the centroid $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$** of the dataset!

---

### Step B: Derivative with Respect to Slope ($m$)

$$\frac{\partial J}{\partial m} = \sum_{i=1}^n 2\Big(y_i - (mx_i + b)\Big)(-x_i) = -2 \sum_{i=1}^n x_i(y_i - mx_i - b) = 0$$

Substitute $b = \bar{y} - m\bar{x}$:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n x_i \Big(y_i - mx_i - (\bar{y} - m\bar{x})\Big) = 0$$

$$\sum_{i=1}^n x_i \Big((y_i - \bar{y}) - m(x_i - \bar{x})\Big) = 0$$

$$\sum_{i=1}^n x_i(y_i - \bar{y}) = m \sum_{i=1}^n x_i(x_i - \bar{x})$$

Using the algebraic identity $\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y}) = \sum x_i(y_i - \bar{y})$:

$$\mathbf{m^* = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2} = \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\text{Var}(X)}}$$

## 4. The Matrix Formulation: The Normal Equation

When scaling from a single feature $x$ to multiple features $\mathbf{x} = [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_d]^T$, scalar calculus becomes cumbersome. We express the entire system in compact linear algebra notation.

Let $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (d+1)}$ be the design matrix with an prepended column of $1$s for the bias, $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ the target vector, and $\boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^{d+1}$ the parameter vector:

$$\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_{1,1} & \dots & x_{1,d} \\ 1 & x_{2,1} & \dots & x_{2,d} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_{n,1} & \dots & x_{n,d} \end{bmatrix}, \quad \boldsymbol{\beta} = \begin{bmatrix} b \\ w_1 \\ \vdots \\ w_d \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ \vdots \\ y_n \end{bmatrix}$$

The residual vector is $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}$, and the loss function becomes:

$$J(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2 = (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})^T (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})$$

Expanding the quadratic form:

$$J(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{y} - 2\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} + \boldsymbol{\beta}^T \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X} \boldsymbol{\beta}$$

Taking matrix derivative with respect to $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ and setting to zero:

$$\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} J(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = -2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} + 2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{0}$$

$$\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$$

Assuming $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ is full rank (invertible), we obtain the famous **Normal Equation**:

$$\mathbf{\hat{\beta} = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}}$$

### Geometric Interpretation: Orthogonal Projection
The product $\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}$ is a linear combination of the columns of $\mathbf{X}$, lying in the column space $\text{col}(\mathbf{X})$. The optimal prediction $\mathbf{\hat{y}} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{\hat{\beta}}$ is the **orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{y}$ onto the subspace spanned by $\mathbf{X}$**. The residual vector $\mathbf{e}$ is strictly orthogonal ($\perp$) to every column in $\mathbf{X}$, meaning $\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}$.

## 5. Goodness of Fit: Deconstructing $R^2$

How well does the fitted line explain the variation in the dataset? We partition the total sum of squares:

```
Total Variation (SST) = Explained Variation (SSR_model) + Unexplained Variation (SSE)
     ∑ (y_i - ȳ)²     =        ∑ (ŷ_i - ȳ)²          +        ∑ (y_i - ŷ_i)²
```

The coefficient of determination, $R^2$, measures the proportion of total variance explained by the model:

$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{SS}_{\text{res}}}{\text{SS}_{\text{tot}}} = 1 - \frac{\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2}$$

- **$R^2 = 1.0$**: Perfect fit; all points lie directly on the line.
- **$R^2 = 0.0$**: The model performs no better than simply predicting the mean $\bar{y}$ for every sample.
- **$R^2 < 0.0$**: The model is worse than the horizontal mean baseline (possible with arbitrary manual guesses).

When $d$ scales to millions of features, inverting the matrix becomes computationally impossible.
