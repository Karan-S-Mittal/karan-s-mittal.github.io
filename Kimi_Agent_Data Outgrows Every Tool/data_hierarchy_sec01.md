It was past midnight when Priya added the 847th conditional. The file had started as twelve lines of clean logic when she joined the fraud team. Now it sprawled across four thousand lines of nested IF-THEN-ELSE statements, each one a scar from a breach encoded into the ruleset guarding the bank's payment rails. She was adding a rule to catch a fraud pattern she had only spotted because the last batch of rules fired wrong, producing yet another false positive that demanded yet another exception. Somewhere on the seventh floor, ATMs were still dispensing cash, each transaction passing through code nobody fully understood.

When judgment is overwhelmed by volume, the reflex is to freeze expertise into rules that execute without sleep or bias. The instinct runs deep. In 1965, Edward Feigenbaum's team at Stanford encoded the heuristics of Nobel-winning chemists into DENDRAL, a program that identified unknown molecules through IF-THEN rules alone, proving that knowledge — not raw computation — was the key to intelligent behavior[^2^]. Seven years later, Edward Shortliffe built MYCIN: roughly six hundred rules that diagnosed bacterial infections and recommended antibiotics with a 65% acceptability rate matching human specialists[^4^]. MYCIN could answer "Why do you believe this?" and trace its reasoning, an explainability that neural networks still struggle to match[^4^]. It never treated a single patient. Liability concerns and the burden of keeping six hundred hand-coded rules current against evolving medical knowledge kept it in the lab — technically brilliant, practically stillborn[^4^].

If MYCIN was the cautionary tale, XCON was the triumph — until it wasn't. Built for Digital Equipment Corporation in 1978, XCON started with 250 rules that configured computer orders. It grew to over 10,000 by the late 1980s, saved DEC $25 to $40 million annually, and reduced fulfillment time from 10–15 weeks to 2–3 days[^5^][^6^]. Nearly two-thirds of Fortune 500 companies followed suit by the mid-1980s[^10^]. But DEC's maintenance team grew from two to eight engineers, and 40% of the rule base changed every year[^7^]. Updating one rule for a new CPU type triggered cascading failures across distant modules, producing what the team called a "rat's nest" of special-case rules[^7^]. The brittleness was structural: deterministic logic performs flawlessly inside its domain and fails catastrophically at the edges, because it cannot handle what it has not been explicitly told to expect[^13^].

Rules persist for good reason. A modern rule engine sustains nine thousand requests per second at 0.23 millisecond median latency — speed no learning system can match[^16^]. Ninety-five percent of ATM transactions still run on COBOL code executing logic written decades ago[^17^]. A regulator auditing a declined transaction can trace the decision to an exact IF-THEN statement, satisfying GDPR Article 22 and related regulatory audit requirements in a single stroke[^26^]. The average mid-market bank maintains 300–800 active fraud detection rules, catching 78–85% of known fraud typologies[^20^]. Rules are fast, cheap, and legally unimpeachable.

But the crack widens with every new dimension. Fraud evolves; rules do not. When a novel pattern emerges, the system is blind until analysts notice the losses and deploy a new rule — a process that takes 4–6 weeks, while losses accumulate and the team plays catch-up[^20^][^19^]. False positive rates hit 85–95%, meaning analysts chase hundreds of phantom alerts for every real threat[^20^]. The combinatorial explosion is merciless: each new rule interacts with others in ways that grow exponentially more complex[^25^]. Five structural flaws define the boundary — brittleness at the edges, the knowledge acquisition bottleneck, the maintenance nightmare of interacting rules, combinatorial explosion as dimensions increase, and the fundamental inability to learn[^13^]. When the space of possible conditions exceeds human capacity to enumerate them, the ruleset collapses under its own weight. The question that follows is deceptively simple: what if, instead of writing every rule by hand, the system could discover patterns itself?
