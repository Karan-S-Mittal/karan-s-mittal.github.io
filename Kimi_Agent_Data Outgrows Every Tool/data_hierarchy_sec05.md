The bank manager with the red pen never truly departed. His intuition calcified into business rules still executing on mainframes worldwide — 95 percent of ATM transactions still flow through COBOL logic written decades ago[^17^], catching obvious fraud at 0.23 millisecond latency[^16^]. The ruleset still runs because twenty cases in every thousand are unambiguous, and encoding them explicitly costs nothing and explains everything to a regulator. XGBoost still scores the remaining flows at 1.5 million per second in production benchmarks, its model weighing less than a megabyte, drawing decision boundaries with a precision no rule could enumerate[^3^][^21^]. The convolutional neural network still reads the receipt the scanner captured, finding patterns where feature engineering cannot reach[^70^]. And the large language model still parses the complaint that arrived in broken Spanish at 2 a.m., because semantic space alone can hold that messiness[^37^].

Economics make the case more brutally than any architecture diagram. Rules cost nothing to execute — a hard-coded threshold consumes microseconds of CPU time with zero marginal cost and never degrades in accuracy over time. XGBoost runs on commodity hardware that amortizes to pennies per million predictions. Deep learning rents GPU time at $8.40 to $19.20 per hour[^60^]. GPT-4's training consumed $78 million in compute[^37^]. The cost gradient alone dictates tool selection: rules execute at near-zero marginal cost, while a 70-billion-parameter model costs nearly $80 per million tokens. That is a cost span of greater than 10,000-fold from rule to transformer[^3^], and the choice is dictated by the shape of the problem, not the prestige of the tool.

Production systems have already internalized this logic. At PayPal and Feedzai, the pipeline operates as a relay, not a sprint. Deep neural networks — LSTMs and transformers trained on months of transaction history — generate dense behavioral embeddings offline, compressing a customer's lifetime activity into a fixed-length vector that captures temporal patterns no human could engineer[^19^]. XGBoost then consumes those pre-computed embeddings at inference time, pairing them with real-time transaction features — amount, merchant category, velocity flags — to render a fraud score in under ten milliseconds. The heavy model does the heavy thinking asynchronously; the light model does the light deciding synchronously. Neither replaces the other. Each layer owns exactly what it was built for. Recent architecture surveys confirm what practitioners already knew: the monolithic "one model to rule them all" approach is effectively dead, replaced by distributed systems where specialized models work in concert[^8^].

The hierarchy was never a ladder to climb. It is a map to read — a terrain of tools organized by the shape of data, the speed of decisions, and the weight of cost. The engineer who understands all four layers doesn't ask "which is best?" They ask "what shape is my data today?" The red pen still moves — but now it draws the map, not the boundary.
